What to study after an MBA, and the deadline that decides it

Dave asked about two UCD courses. Here is the honest answer to that, plus two accredited options at NCI that are cheaper, at a higher level, and closing this week. Every figure below was checked against the live pages today.

days. Applications for the funded NCI micro-credentials close Sunday 31 August 2026. Nothing else on this page matters as much as that date.

The NCI courses are 80% subsidised, which is why they cost less than the UCD ones despite sitting at a higher academic level. That subsidy has strict residency rules, and they decide whether this student is looking at a bill of about €300 or about €1,400.

If they are Irish or EU, living here

Needs a PPS number, full-time residence in the Republic, EU, EEA, UK or Swiss nationality, and ordinary residence in that area for three of the last five years.

Subsidy applies The NCI options below are the clear value, and it is not close.

If they came to Ireland to study

Time held on a Stamp 2 student visa counts for nothing toward the three-year residency test. A non-EU applicant needs Stamp 4, 5 or 6 and 36 months of reckonable residency.

Subsidy likely does not apply Full NCI fees are €1,300 to €1,500, and the comparison with UCD becomes genuinely close.

One rule worth a phone call, not a guess

Springboard+ says that someone who has recently graduated from a Level 8 honours degree must wait a year before enrolling on a Level 9 funded course. An MBA is a Level 9 award, not a Level 8, so on a plain reading this does not apply here. That is somebody else's regulation and not mine to interpret, so it is worth thirty seconds with NCI admissions before anyone fills in a form.

Full criteria: springboardcourses.ie/eligibility. Eligibility is assessed on the course start date and proof is required before enrolment.

Both are QQI-accredited minor awards at NFQ Level 9, the same level as a master's, carrying real ECTS credits that can be recognised if the student later enrols on a full master's at NCI.

Which of the two

Big Data and AI in Business is the closer match to what Dave originally asked about. It builds hands-on competence in interpreting datasets using SQL and Python, taught step by step with AI tools alongside, and NCI states plainly that no prior technical background is required. The emphasis sits on the judgement half: framing the problem, testing model assumptions, governance under the EU AI Act, and communicating the result. Credits can be recognised toward NCI's MSc in Business Analytics for Decision Makers.

AI Technologies is the broader survey of what the technologies are and where they apply, rather than a course where you build things. Its credits point toward the Postgraduate Diploma and MSc in AI for Business.

For someone straight out of an MBA who wants to be able to do the work rather than only talk about it, Big Data and AI in Business is the one I would look at first. It is also the cheaper of the two and the earlier deadline in practice, because it starts first.

Two I am not recommending, and why

  • Certificate in AI Strategy, Governance and Leadership. On paper this was the best fit of the four: Level 9, 20 ECTS, written for senior managers and non-technical leaders. Its NCI page now says "funded places have reached full capacity". Without the subsidy it is €3,000. Worth an email to NCI in case a place frees up, but do not build a plan on it.
  • Certificate in Emerging Digital Technologies. Genuinely good value at €340 for 20 ECTS, but it is an NFQ Level 7 award. Somebody holding a Level 9 MBA would be studying below the level they already hold. Wrong direction.
Apply

Both applications run through the HEA portal at springboardcourses.ie, not through NCI directly. Entry requirement for both is a 2.2 honours degree at Level 8 or equivalent, which an MBA holder clears comfortably. The €260 or €300 contribution is not due until 13 November, so applying now does not mean paying now.

Both are real, both are well regarded, and one of them does something no NCI course on this page does. There is one structural fact worth knowing before comparing prices, and it comes from UCD rather than from me.

"With their tight focus and duration, UCD Professional Academy Diplomas and Certificates are stand-alone certifications and are not referenced to NFQ (National Framework of Qualifications) levels or UCD University academic courses. They are not generally accepted for transfer credit onto academic courses, but are widely recognised and valued by employers."

UCD Professional Academy, Certifications and Governance page

So the qualification does not sit on the national framework and does not carry credit that transfers into a degree. That is not a criticism. UCD is upfront about it and states the trade-off in its own words, which happen to be the cleanest decision rule on this page:

"Professional Academy Diplomas are suitable for career minded learners wishing to advance their professional skills and prospects rather than their academic credentials."

Three things worth correcting gently

  • The Microsoft 365 one is not a Diploma. Its official title is "Automate Your Work with Microsoft 365 and Power Automate". Small thing, but it matters if the student is putting it on a CV.
  • Its page does not state the session length, so the total contact hours cannot be worked out. The Data Analysis course is 12 sessions of three hours, which comes to 36 hours of teaching.
  • The €760 rate on the Data Analysis course is a Skillnet member price, through BioPharmaChem Skillnet. If the student's employer is in a Skillnet network, that is a serious discount and it changes the maths below. Worth asking.
Where UCD wins outright

If the actual need is automating workflows inside a Microsoft shop, Power Automate and Power Apps, then the UCD course is the answer and nothing at NCI competes with it. Same if the need is specifically Power BI and Tableau on the CV, because those tool names appear in job adverts and "SQL and Python" is a different line on a different kind of advert.

Easy to confuse

UCD Professional Academy and UCD Innovation Academy are separate things. The Innovation Academy does run credit-bearing Level 9 awards. If someone has been told "UCD does an accredited diploma", that may be what they mean.

If the subsidy applies

€260 for a Level 9 accredited award carrying 10 ECTS that can count toward a master's, against €1,695 for a certificate that its own institution says is not referenced to the framework and does not generally transfer as credit. That is roughly six and a half times the price for the qualification that carries less academic weight. On those numbers the NCI route is not a close call.

If the subsidy does not apply

Full NCI fees are €1,300 for Big Data and AI in Business and €1,500 for AI Technologies, against €1,695 at UCD or €760 at the Skillnet rate. Now it is a real decision. NCI still carries the accreditation and the credits. UCD still teaches the specific tools by name and, at €760, would be the cheaper option by some distance. Either choice is defensible, and it turns on what the student is actually buying.

The thing I would want the student to hear anyway

"Without coding" was a strong differentiator in about 2019. It meant you could do the analysis despite not being able to program. In 2026 the layer that removes the coding is the AI itself: you describe the analysis and the model writes and runs it. So the scarce skill has moved. It is no longer operating the tool without writing Python; it is framing the right question and knowing when the answer is wrong. Somebody with a fresh MBA is unusually well placed to own that half, and it is the half a pure tools course does not teach. This is an argument about emphasis, not a reason to avoid learning Power BI, which remains genuinely hireable.

Nobody has asked the student any of these yet, and each one changes the answer.

  1. Irish or EU with three years' residency here, or an international graduate? This is the price question. It decides whether the NCI route costs €260 or €1,300, and it is the difference between an obvious answer and a genuine dilemma. Everything else waits on it.
  2. Does this need to count toward something later, or is a certificate enough? If there is any chance of a master's or further accredited study, ECTS at Level 9 are worth real money later and a non-framework diploma is a dead end academically. If the goal is simply a recognised line on a CV and a skill in hand by Christmas, UCD's own framing applies and their course is a fair choice.
  3. Do they want to build the thing, or lead the people who build it? Build the dashboard or automate the workflow, and it is UCD's tools courses or NCI's Big Data certificate. Direct the work, judge whether the output is trustworthy, and answer for it, and that is the Level 9 strategic material. An MBA graduate is often reaching for the first when their next role will pay them for the second.
Declared

I lecture at NCI. Two of the four courses recommended here are my own employer's. I have tried to make that visible rather than invisible: UCD's strengths are quoted in UCD's words, the case where UCD wins is stated plainly, and the branch where the NCI price advantage disappears is set out in full. Read it with that in mind and discount it accordingly.

Where sources disagree

NCI's printed 2026 micro-credentials booklet is behind the live pages in three places. The booklet gives 22 September as the start for AI Technologies where the portal says 29 September; it gives 14 September for Big Data and AI in Business where the portal's date field says 17 September, though that same live page's own timetable text still says the week commencing 14 September; and it gives 19 October for the face-to-face Saturday where the portal says 17 October. This page uses the live figures throughout. Every NCI page also carries the line that schedules are indicative and subject to change, so confirm dates before arranging anything around them.

What could not be confirmed

  • Whether the funded places on the two open certificates are still genuinely available. The pages show a future deadline and a live Apply button with no "full" notice, but no page publishes a count of remaining places, and one course has already filled.
  • Calendar start dates for the UCD courses beyond the weekday and time. Those load through a script that could not be read.
  • Total contact hours for the Microsoft 365 course. The page does not state the session length.

The 36-hour figure for the UCD Data Analysis course is arithmetic from 12 weeks at three hours, not a number either page states.
